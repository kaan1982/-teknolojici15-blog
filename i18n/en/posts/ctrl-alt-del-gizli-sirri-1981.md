When a computer freezes, your fingers go almost by reflex to the same three keys: Ctrl, Alt and Delete. The combination is so familiar that it has become a kind of "SOS" signal. But it was never actually designed for ordinary users.

## 1981, Boca Raton: the engineers' shortcut

Ctrl+Alt+Del was written in 1981 by David Bradley, who worked on the IBM PC project team in Boca Raton, Florida. Bradley was one of the project's lead engineers and the person who developed the computer's ROM-BIOS.

The date should be read with some caution: Wikipedia bases 1981 on a colleague's email from March 2018; the exact day the combination was written isn't known.

The purpose was very practical. Developers and technical writers had to switch the machine off and on every time a program locked up. Bradley's combination restarted the computer in software without cutting power (this is called a "soft reboot"). According to Wikipedia, it was an internal development feature that restarted directly, without asking for a warning or confirmation.

## First there was Ctrl+Alt+Esc

In his first attempt Bradley used the Ctrl+Alt+Esc combination. The problem was that these three keys were close together on the left side of the keyboard, and it was very easy to hit them by accident and restart the computer. A single careless hand movement was enough to lose unsaved work.

According to Wikipedia, Mel Hallerman, the project's lead programmer, suggested Delete in place of Esc as a safety measure. The logic was simple: on the original IBM PC keyboard, Ctrl, Alt and Delete were placed so that they couldn't be pressed together with one hand. In other words, you could only do this combination deliberately, with two hands.

> **Did you know?** That Ctrl+Alt+Del can't be pressed with one hand wasn't a side effect, it was the design itself. The whole purpose of the combination was to make an accidental restart nearly impossible.

## "I may have invented it, but Bill made it famous"

What put Ctrl+Alt+Del into the lives of millions of people was Windows. In Windows NT in particular, the combination was used as a "secure attention sequence" pressed before the login screen. According to Wikipedia, the aim was to prevent malicious software from showing a fake login screen, because this key sequence couldn't be captured by programs. In other words, after pressing Ctrl+Alt+Del you could be sure the login window that appeared really belonged to the operating system. According to Wikipedia, this requirement is off by default in current Windows versions; it is turned on if the computer is joined to a corporate network (a Windows domain).

At an event held at The Tech Museum on August 8, 2001 for the IBM PC's 20th anniversary, Bradley summed this up with a witty line: "I may have invented it, but I think Bill made it famous." Wikipedia reports that Bradley immediately added that this remark referred to the Windows NT login screen.

## Bill Gates's admission: "It was a mistake"

Years later Bill Gates also spoke about the combination. According to Wikipedia, in a Q&A session on September 21, 2013, referring to the use of Ctrl+Alt+Del to log in to Windows, he said "it was a mistake".

The full sentence that Scientific American quoted from the Harvard conversation is even more interesting: "We could have had a single button, but the guy who designed the IBM keyboard didn't want to give us our single button. We programmed at a low level; it was a mistake."

So, according to Gates, the ideal solution was a single button dedicated to logging in. When IBM wasn't keen on that, Microsoft used the existing three-key combination.

## How did an internal tool become culture?

There's an ironic side too: a combination deliberately made hard to press by accident ended up as a key sequence that millions of people press on purpose every day. The designers' worry that "nobody should press this by mistake" evolved in the Windows era into the habit of "everyone should press this every morning".

The story of Ctrl+Alt+Del shows how a small engineering decision can turn into a lasting habit. A few lines of code a team wrote to make its own job easier have lived on keyboards all around the world for more than forty years.

If there had been a single "login" key, as Gates wanted, do you think that key would be this iconic today?
