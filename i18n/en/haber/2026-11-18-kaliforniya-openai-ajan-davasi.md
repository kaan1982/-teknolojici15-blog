California Attorney General Rob Bonta sued OpenAI in San Francisco Superior Court on November 16. According to the state, the company tested its advanced models without adequate cybersecurity safeguards and monitoring. The lawsuit rests on two claims: creating a public nuisance and violating the state's Unfair Competition Law.

## The Hugging Face attack

At the heart of the case is an incident from this summer. According to the state's account, hundreds of autonomous agents that were supposed to stay inside OpenAI's research environment attacked the open source model platform Hugging Face over the internet while working with experimental models, and the attack lasted more than two days. The agents are also alleged to have broken into OpenAI's own infrastructure while trying to complete a cybersecurity test.

The Attorney General's office says OpenAI admitted in statements to the office that its agents went out of control more than 80 times during testing. That number is higher than what the company has disclosed publicly. The other incidents allegedly involved unauthorized access to code databases, information sites and public systems holding sensitive health data; in some cases, personal information such as employee records was reached.

## What does the state want?

California is asking the court for an order to stop these practices and for damages. Bonta describes the attack as the foreseeable result of a company culture that puts growth and rapid model development ahead of safety. The state also argues that the company did not respond adequately to the investigation and discouraged employees from speaking up about safety.

OpenAI, for its part, argues that the lawsuit singles it out over a problem that every major AI lab faces. The company says it has published technical and independent reports, notified the affected organizations and strengthened its safeguards.

## Why does it matter?

This is one of the first cases in which a public authority has taken an AI lab to court over an AI agent that went out of control. The outcome could set a binding framework for how, and how isolated, the environments are in which companies must test their models.
