The Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS), part of the US Department of Commerce, published a new interim rule on November 16. The rule aims to stop Chinese companies from getting around US export restrictions through data centers in third countries.

## What does the rule do?

BIS classified Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil as countries posing an "unacceptable risk of diversion". Sales of the most advanced chips and servers to these five countries now require an export license. Remote access to certain graphics processors and servers running in data centers in the same countries is also prohibited. Commercial recommendation systems used by online stores and social media were left out of scope on the grounds that they carry lower risk.

Chinese firms could not buy the most advanced chips directly, but they could rent computing power from cloud services in countries such as Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand. Between 2021 and 2024, only US-based cloud companies were required not to serve Chinese customers; there was no similar obligation for operators abroad. BIS describes the new step as a "logical extension" of existing restrictions.

## The Nvidia scandal in the background

The rule arrived in the shadow of investigations surrounding Nvidia. The Commerce and Justice departments confirmed that a criminal investigation is under way into Megaspeed, a company that builds data centers in Malaysia. Singapore said it is reviewing the details and is in talks with the US about access for its own companies. Counterpoint analyst Neil Shah says the compliance burden is shifting from chipmakers to cloud operators, which could accelerate Chinese alternatives such as Huawei Ascend. On the day the news broke, Nvidia shares fell 4.1 percent.

## Why does it matter?

The chip war is no longer only about which port the boxes ship to, but also about who connects to which server. A new era of customer screening is beginning for data center investments in Southeast Asia and for the companies that use these centers.
