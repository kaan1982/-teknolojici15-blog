A system the US Central Intelligence Agency uses for open source intelligence analysis has been breached. The source who spoke to the New York Times, which broke the story, said the attack also affected the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI) and the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency. The agencies said classified networks and classified information were not affected and that no other systems were at risk. An ODNI official said there was no interruption to critical capabilities; the incident is reported to have been remediated.

## If it is not classified, why does it matter?

The system that was breached sweeps publicly available sources such as social media, news sites, foreign broadcasts, satellite imagery and purchased commercial data feeds. So the raw material is not a state secret.

Ben Bernstein of the security firm Huntress says attacks on the unclassified side of federal agencies are not rare at all, but that this does not make the incident harmless: anyone who can see what analysts are searching for and which topics they are watching gets a sense of US intelligence priorities and blind spots without touching a single classified file. Bernstein adds that these systems have to stay open to the internet in order to do their job, which is exactly why they are hard to protect.

Who the attacker was has not been disclosed. The first signs in press reports suggest the use of ARTEX, an AI assisted penetration testing tool released as open source this year, and that the trail may lead to China; US officials have not confirmed this.

## Not an isolated incident

In the same days, the Treasury's financial crimes unit FinCEN said it was investigating an incident involving unauthorized access to a limited number of systems, shut some of them down and asked banks to file reports by phone. An alleged breach of the FBI's nationwide crime database NCIC is being examined, the Department of Agriculture shut its systems down for several days, and the NSA's classified network was also down for days last week. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton blames most of the recent outages on hurried patches and upgrades.
