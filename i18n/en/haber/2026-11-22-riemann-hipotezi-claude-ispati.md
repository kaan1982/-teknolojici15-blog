In a four-page note published on his website on November 20, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced that together with the company's AI model Claude he had found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. A day later, the Lean and Mathlib communities confirmed in a joint statement that the proof is correct.

## Why is this so big?

The hypothesis, put forward by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, says that the distribution of prime numbers along the number line, which looks random at first glance, actually follows a deep order. It is one of the Millennium Prize Problems, for which the Clay Mathematics Institute offers a 1 million dollar prize. Alpöge says the method also proves a much broader version, the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis.

## How was the proof verified?

There is no paper for humans to read. According to The Next Web, Alpöge wrote that a readable version ran past 250 thousand pages in his own draft, and he published only the Lean code. Lean is proof software that checks every logical step one by one. According to the Lean community's statement, the library contains 17 million lines of code, was compiled without errors by dozens of people, and two separate tools were used to confirm that the proven theorem is identical to the statement of the Riemann hypothesis in Mathlib. Andrew Sutherland of MIT told Scientific American that the chance of a problem is close to zero.

Reactions are not unanimous. Brian Conrad of Stanford sharply criticized Alpöge for publishing a proof he does not fully understand. Alpöge himself admits that he has not yet mastered the whole argument and that Claude left him far behind in the final steps, and he says he will simplify the proof and write it up. The announcement came one day after Anthropic's stock market debut; Alpöge wrote that the timing was not deliberate.

## Why does it matter?

For the first time, AI appears to have closed the most famous open question in mathematics. It signals a new era in how science will be done from now on, but it will still take time for humans to understand the proof.
