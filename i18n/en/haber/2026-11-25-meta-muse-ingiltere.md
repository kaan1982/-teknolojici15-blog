On November 24, Meta launched Muse, the personal AI agent it opened in the US in September, in the UK. Muse does not just answer questions: you give it a task, and it sends emails, searches for holiday options, fills in forms and, if you allow it, makes purchases.

## How does it work?

Muse runs on Muse Spark, the first model from Meta Superintelligence Labs. According to Meta, each user's Muse runs on a virtual computer in the cloud set aside for that user; the agent does not see passwords or card details and asks for approval before important steps such as sending an email or making a payment. Payments go through Stripe's Onelink wallet or Shopify's Shop Pay service. The app is open to people over 18 and works on iOS, Android, the web and WhatsApp; basic use is free, with a subscription for more.

In the UK, Meta has partnered with brands such as Marks & Spencer, Currys, Sainsbury's, ASOS, Trainline and easyJet.

## The snags

BBC technology editor Zoe Kleinman tried the agent for a week. Muse summarized emails and suggested replies, and it found a train ticket but could not buy it from the cheaper app she uses. Amazon had blocked Muse from its site, citing data security; not knowing this, the agent told Kleinman it had been waiting an hour for the page to load.

The UK's data protection regulator, the ICO, said it had reminded Meta of its personal data responsibilities before the launch, and told consumers that agents can make mistakes and that those mistakes can grow when they act on their own.

## Why does it matter?

Meta is spending about 145 billion dollars this year and expects that investment to pay off through agents like Muse that everyone will use. Amazon's block, meanwhile, shows that big platforms do not want other companies' agents inside their stores.
