The unpublished notebooks of Kurt Gödel, who transformed mathematics with his incompleteness theorems, have been read from start to finish for the first time with the help of AI. According to a report published by The Guardian on 13 November, logician Jan von Plato of the University of Helsinki used Anthropic's Claude model to extract 663 candidate theorems from the notebooks.

## What is in the notebooks?

The material consists of 16 working notebooks Gödel wrote in the early 1940s, 1,009 pages in total. The notes are dense logic and mathematics written in a German shorthand called Gabelsberger, which had already fallen out of use even in Gödel's lifetime. Von Plato is one of very few people who can read the script, and since 2017 he had been able to progress at a rate of about one page a week.

In September he gave Claude his transcriptions, his own books on Gödel and secondary sources. Within a few days he received a 650-page commentary and a list of 663 theorems. According to von Plato, when the list was run through the formal proof checker Coq, about 400 theorems were verified as they stood, about 200 needed corrections or additional assumptions, and about 50 turned out to be false. Gödel had marked some of these in the margin as "falsch" (false).

## The most striking finding

Gödel claimed to have proved in the 1940s the independence of the axiom of choice, the result for which Paul Cohen won the Fields Medal in 1966, but he never presented the proof. According to von Plato, the proof is almost fully there in the notebooks; a single definition is faulty, and the corrected version passed the Coq check. Joel David Hamkins of the University of Notre Dame says the result would be remarkable if confirmed, but that historians will have to determine how much of the work belongs to Gödel and how much to Claude. No paper or independent review has been published yet.

## Why it matters

Here AI did less to produce new mathematics than to shrink an archival task too big for a human lifetime down to a few days. But a statement that passes formal verification does not prove what Gödel actually thought; the questions of attribution and verification remain open.
