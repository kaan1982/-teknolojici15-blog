The US Federal Communications Commission gave SpaceX conditional permission on Wednesday to operate data centers in orbit. The licence covers placing up to 1 million satellites carrying AI chips in a project the company calls Starmind. It is the regulator's first approval of a satellite constellation for AI data centers in space.

## The timetable and the technical plan

The FCC has allowed plenty of time: SpaceX must put its first working satellite in orbit by 2033 and complete all 1 million by 2040. The satellites are planned for altitudes between 500 and 1,002 kilometers.

They will be linked to each other and to Starlink by laser; the FCC's approval allows them to be controlled from the ground over radio frequency if the laser link fails. SpaceX describes the project as collecting solar energy in space to feed AI on the ground, and plans to make the chips at its Terafab plant.

## The objections still stand

Amazon, Viasat and the American Astronomical Society had opposed the application, citing space debris, orbital crowding, radio frequency interference and environmental impact. Amazon called the filing not a real plan but an exercise in high ambition. The FCC rejected some objections, such as the claim of harm to scientific research, as outside its own remit.

Not everything is settled: the regulator put on hold, for now, a request that satellites above 600 kilometers be moved to graveyard orbits at the end of their lives, saying it had not been given enough information. Problems such as building and launching the satellites cheaply and cooling the chips in space also remain.

## Why does it matter?

As AI's electricity and cooling bill grows on the ground, companies are looking up. Google launched its first server satellite last month with Project Suncatcher; Blue Origin will make its first heavy launch in 2029. This approval is the moment computing in space moved off the slide deck and onto a regulator's calendar.
