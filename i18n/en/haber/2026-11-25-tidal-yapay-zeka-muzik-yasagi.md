On November 24, the music streaming platform Tidal announced that it will ban songs made entirely with AI. The ban starts on December 9; after that date, neither individual uploaders nor distribution companies will be able to add such tracks to the platform.

## A label was not enough

Since July 2026, Tidal has put an "AI" badge on songs made entirely with AI and paid no royalties on them. According to the company, that step did not stop the flood: over the last 90 days, 43 percent of new tracks arriving on the platform were fully AI-generated. According to Ars Technica, on November 6 the share briefly hit 60 percent for a day.

Today Tidal has about 27 million AI-labeled tracks, 6.3 percent of its catalog. All of them will be deleted. As reported by TechCrunch, the number of tracks removed by the end of the year could exceed 29 million, and under the new rule the platform will turn away 80,000 to 85,000 tracks a day. The company says 96 percent of the songs to be deleted were not played even once in the last 90 days. In addition, 66,000 tracks had already been removed for impersonating real artists.

## Is using AI banned?

No. The ban only covers tracks produced by AI from start to finish; songs that use AI as a tool but carry human contribution will stay. Tidal has not explained how it will detect them. Ars Technica writes that 94 percent of the labeled tracks came from Suno, which adds a watermark, so watermark scanning could catch most of them, but open models running locally could slip through that net.

## Why does it matter?

While Spotify, YouTube Music and Apple Music keep AI music online with labels, Tidal has become, after Bandcamp, one of the first major platforms to go for an outright ban. In the fight between AI "slop" and real artists' royalty income, this decision will be a test for other services.
