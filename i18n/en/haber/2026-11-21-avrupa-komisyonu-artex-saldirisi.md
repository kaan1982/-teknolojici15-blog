The European Commission, the executive body of the European Union, confirmed on November 19 that it had been hit by a cyberattack that began in October. Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier described the incident as "an AI-generated cyber campaign".

## What happened?

According to The Record, the first traces of the attack were spotted on October 22. The attack disrupted the Commission's staff systems for more than 10 days. As reported by The Register, Sysper, which employees use for payroll, working hours and leave, and MyPMO, which handles health reimbursements, were temporarily shut down; translation services were also affected. The systems came back online this week, but Sysper crashed once again on Wednesday. Salaries were reportedly paid on time.

According to Politico, which first broke the story, CERT-EU, the cybersecurity team of the EU institutions, found that the attackers used ARTEX, an open source, AI-assisted penetration testing tool. The tool uses commercial AI models to automate the process of finding and exploiting vulnerabilities. Last week the FBI announced that ARTEX was being used by both criminals and state-backed groups, and that in some attacks it took less than three hours to go from first contact with a target to privileged access.

Regnier said there was no evidence of a data breach broadly affecting staff and that they were not aware of any ransom demand. The investigation is ongoing. This is the third cyber incident the Commission has disclosed this year.

## Why does it matter?

Putting AI agents to work on the attack side means jobs that used to take days now take hours. Finding traces of such a tool in one of an institution's best-protected systems shows that defense needs to automate at the same speed.
