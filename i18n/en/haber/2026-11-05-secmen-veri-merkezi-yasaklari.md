In the US midterm elections on 3 November, AI data centers went to the ballot box and suffered a heavy defeat. According to a CNBC analysis based on Ballotpedia data, 41 local proposals restricting data centers in various ways were put to a vote in 10 states. In almost every vote with a known result, voters sided against data centers. The only exception was the town of Harwood in North Dakota.

## Bans, moratoriums, power rules

The Washington Post wrote that proposals banning or limiting data centers passed in at least 38 local governments; the difference in figures comes from votes being counted in different ways. Voters' objections centered on noise, electricity and water use. More than half of the proposals, 21 votes, were in Ohio. Ohio is also the state where OpenAI aims to build one of the world's largest planned AI campuses.

The issue did not stop at local votes. According to CNBC, Democrat Amy Acton, who won the governorship in Ohio, supports a conditional moratorium on data centers. In Texas, Gina Hinojosa beat the incumbent governor while criticizing the rapid spread of data centers. In CNBC's voter survey, 56 percent of respondents said data centers were an important factor in their decision.

## The tech world is uneasy

Even before the election, AI stocks were under pressure from interest rate and overspending worries. Big cloud companies spend hundreds of billions of dollars a year on infrastructure. Some Democrats, who won a majority in Congress, have called for AI company executives to testify under oath.

## Why it matters

The AI race is won with electricity, land and local permits as much as with chips and models. If voters shut the door, the facility to train the best model cannot be built. In the coming months, where and on what terms companies build new campuses will be decisive.
